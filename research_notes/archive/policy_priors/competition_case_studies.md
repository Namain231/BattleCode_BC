# Case studies: how top teams in AI programming competitions combined rules, imitation learning, RL and search

Scope note: many Kaggle writeup pages (kaggle.com/competitions/*/writeups/*) render with JavaScript and returned only a
title when fetched, so for several Kaggle solutions the details below come from mirrors (GitHub READMEs, company blogs,
Japanese summary articles) or from search-result snippets of the Kaggle page. Where only a snippet was available this is
marked "(snippet)". Medium (Battlesnake post-mortem) was blocked by Cloudflare; the OpenReview Lux S3 paper page showed a
bot check. These are listed as gaps rather than guessed.

## Q1. Kaggle Hungry Geese (2021, 4-player snake on a 7x11 torus): how the top teams combined IL, RL and search

### Takeaway
The winner (DeNA's HandyRL team) used large-scale off-policy RL self-play (16M games over 3 weeks), then 4 more days
against a varied pool of past and hand-written aggressive opponents, and at play time ran the network together with
Decoupled-UCT MCTS. The 3rd and 11th places started with imitation learning on top leaderboard episodes (score > 1200),
fine-tuned with RL, and searched with MCTS at play time. Pure RL without search, or RL without enough compute, did
noticeably worse. Evaluating agents offline was the main pain point for mid-table teams.

### Cited Findings
- 1st place: DeNA team "HandyRL" (Kazuki/Ikki Tanaka and Katsumi Ohwatari/Odo), 1st of 875 teams (1,039 participants). —
  [DeNA news](https://dena.com/jp/news/4774/); [dena.ai news](https://dena.ai/news/kaggle-hungry-geese/)
- HandyRL's winning method: off-policy policy-gradient RL; "3 weeks of self-play learning through 16 million matches",
  then "4 days of additional learning through 3 million matches" against varied opponents, including past models and
  deliberately aggressive agents. — [DeNA Engineering blog](https://engineering.dena.com/blog/2021/12/distributed-reinforcement-learning-with-handyrl/)
- Network: a 3x3 conv net (Go/shogi style) with torus padding (values from the opposite edge). One network outputs the
  actions and expected rankings for all 4 geese. Inputs: per-player 8x7x11 planes plus global 5x7x11 planes; remaining steps
  encoded at several scales through tanh. — [DeNA Engineering blog](https://engineering.dena.com/blog/2021/12/distributed-reinforcement-learning-with-handyrl/)
- At play time the winner ensembled "two types of agents: deep reinforcement learning and Monte Carlo tree search",
  using Decoupled UCT for simultaneous moves and Go-engine board tricks to reach "thousands to tens of thousands of
  simulations per second". MCTS was added because it "recognize[s] checkmate positions and endgame calculations that deep
  models miss". The team was 2nd one day before the end. — [DeNA Engineering blog](https://engineering.dena.com/blog/2021/12/distributed-reinforcement-learning-with-handyrl/)
- HandyRL's publicly shared Hungry Geese model took about 1 day on 1 GPU + 64 CPUs to train. The team won the February
  monthly prize early in the competition and open-sourced the framework, which then dominated the early leaderboard. —
  [dena.ai HandyRL release](https://dena.ai/news/202103-handyrl/) (snippet); [Zenn retrospective](https://zenn.dev/ktechb/articles/e2394bc27358c4)
- 3rd place (Maxwell, LB 1239.1): "Behavior Cloning Reinforcement Learning Agent with modified MCTS". ResNet
  policy/value net (8 layers, 46 channels), 20 engineered features including flood fill. Repeated cycles: collect episodes
  from strong public agents (score > 1200) plus episodes generated by the MCTS agents, then imitation-learn on the updated
  set "reflecting current metagame". V-trace targets. Compute: A100 + 16 CPUs to train, 12x 1080Ti to generate episodes,
  224 GCP CPUs to evaluate. — [Speaker Deck, hoxomaxwell](https://speakerdeck.com/hoxomaxwell/kaggle-hungry-geese)
- 11th place (NN+MCTS): 3-stage training: imitation learning on episodes from agents rated above 1230, then HandyRL
  self-play fine-tuning, then imitation learning again; MCTS at play time. — [Kaggle 11th place writeup](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/hungry-geese/writeups/the-wisdom-of-manjushri-if-three-ducks-approach-11) (snippet)
- Another top solution combined a HandyRL-trained network with a Stockfish-like lookahead and a flood fill for area
  control and move desirability. — [digitalspecialists/hungrygeese](https://github.com/digitalspecialists/hungrygeese) (snippet)
- 21st place (silver) retrospective: improved the HandyRL baseline with a head-to-tail gradient body encoding (shows which
  segments vacate soon), step/hunger channels, and a mix of past agents instead of pure self-play (to escape local
  optima). Reached 9th, then stalled, then added MCTS in the last month and finished 21st. A baseline took ~1 week on an
  i5 + consumer GPU. The central complaint: "high variance in win counts across trials and complex matchup dynamics made
  offline evaluation nearly impossible with limited resources", which led to ineffective micro-tweaks. — [Zenn retrospective](https://zenn.dev/ktechb/articles/e2394bc27358c4)
- Value-based DQN variants did poorly: an academic study found convergence "difficult due to random geese initialization and
  food generation". — [arXiv 2109.01954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01954)
- 2nd place "Goosebumps": writeup exists but the content could not be retrieved. — [Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/hungry-geese/writeups/goosebumps-goosebumps-solution-2nd-place)

### Inferences
- The pattern that won at the top of Hungry Geese is: **network prior (from RL, or IL then RL) plus explicit search at play
  time**. Search covered the tactical "certain death / forced kill" cases that networks miss. This matches the Viper
  design (network log-prob added inside a C++ safety class). The obvious next step for Viper is short-horizon search
  guided by the network prior (e.g. decoupled UCT or paranoid search over nearby heads), rather than more network training.
- The 3rd-place loop (imitate the current top meta, generate new episodes with the search agent, re-imitate) is a cheap
  "expert iteration" that stays anchored to strong play. It is the IL analogue of the network self-imitation Viper tried.
  The difference is that the 3rd-place team imitated **search-improved** play, not the raw network's winning moves.
- The RL winner used ~16M+3M games and a GPU plus 64 CPUs for weeks. Viper's PPO runs were orders of magnitude
  smaller, so their failure is consistent with the compute scale of known RL successes, not evidence that RL cannot work.

### Gaps
- 2nd place (Goosebumps) method not retrieved (Kaggle page JS-only).
- Exact HandyRL tricks (UPGO/V-trace/TD-lambda settings, how the MCTS and NN were ensembled at play time) are not detailed
  beyond the blog; the Kaggle discussion was not readable.

## Q2. Battlesnake: what the top AIs do

### Takeaway
Battlesnake's strong snakes are mostly tree search (minimax/alpha-beta, paranoid or MaxN for more than 2 players, some MCTS)
with flood-fill and length/food heuristics as the evaluation. One documented ML success (Asymptotic Labs) reportedly topped
the global arena using a desktop GPU in under a week, after the team felt alpha-beta had hit its limit under the 500 ms/turn
budget. Its details could not be read.

### Cited Findings
- Minimax is "a popular algorithm in Battlesnake": a tree search that looks several moves ahead and scores end states with
  an evaluation function. — [coreyja.com, Minimax in Battlesnake](https://coreyja.com/posts/BattlesnakeMinimax/Minimax%20in%20Battlesnake/) (snippet; full page reset the connection)
- A stateless minimax + alpha-beta snake won 1st at the RBC AI snake competition. — [calvinl4/battlesnake-winter-2022](https://github.com/calvinl4/battlesnake-winter-2022) (snippet)
- Academic work applies MaxN (a multi-player minimax variant) and locality-based adversarial N-player search to Battlesnake,
  and simultaneous/adversarial MCTS to the CoG AI Snakes competition. — [GI paper, N-player search using locality](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/358e228c-0917-4849-a4fe-40d155c3232c/content) (snippet); [rkthomps/MCTSSnake](https://github.com/rkthomps/MCTSSnake) (snippet)
- Asymptotic Labs post-mortem, "Using a desktop GPU to top the global arena": in earlier years they succeeded with
  minimax/alpha-beta, but "given the 500ms per turn limit" felt it was maxed out. They rejected a supervised model trained on
  alpha-beta games (too slow to generate quality games; "would likely just converge to the same strategy") and went
  RL/self-play. — [Asymptotic Labs, Medium](https://medium.com/asymptoticlabs/battlesnake-post-mortem-a5917f9a3428) (snippet only; the page returned 403 and a Cloudflare block)
- Open AlphaZero-style Battlesnake implementations exist (e.g. randomized synchronous parallel MCTS, no "pit" evaluation
  step). — [AlphaSnake-Zero](https://github.com/Fool-Yang/AlphaSnake-Zero) (snippet)

### Inferences
- Viper's safety class, which forbids head-on-losing and trapped moves, amounts to a depth-1 or depth-2 search. Battlesnake
  experience suggests deeper paranoid search restricted to dragons near our head would pay off. Viper's CPU budget
  (~100M/turn, ~23M used) leaves room for it.
- The Asymptotic Labs remark that a network trained on search games "would converge to the same strategy" matches Viper's
  finding: imitation caps near the teacher (~72% accuracy ceiling).

### Gaps
- No official Battlesnake leaderboard analysis naming the top snakes' algorithms was found. The Asymptotic Labs details
  (network, MCTS settings, compute, how long it stayed on top) could not be read.

## Q3. Lux AI Season 1 (2021), Season 2 (2023), Season 3 (NeurIPS 2024)

### Takeaway
- **S1:** Deep RL won. Toad Brigade (IMPALA + UPGO + TD-lambda, a 20M-parameter ResNet, curriculum from small to large
  networks, KL to a frozen teacher) abandoned its own rule bot within the first month. Other teams then imitation-learned
  from Toad Brigade's replays and reached upper-leaderboard ranks, not the top.
- **S2:** Rules won. The 1st and 2nd places were rule-based bots; RL teams placed around 4th.
- **S3 (partial observability):** RL won. The 1st place (Flat Neurons) used IMPALA with ~200M parameters on 8xH100 for 3–4
  days; 2nd (Frog Parade, including S1 Toad Brigade members) used PPO on consumer GPUs for 8 days. The best rule-based bot
  was about 20th. Top teams used **win/loss-only rewards** and **past-model / PFSP opponent pools**.

### Cited Findings
- Toad Brigade (S1): fully convolutional ResNet, 24 residual blocks, ~20M parameters, 128-channel 5x5 convs, squeeze-
  excitation, no batch norm; 3 actor heads plus a critic. IMPALA with UPGO and TD-lambda losses, plus "KL regularization
  against a frozen teacher model" to stabilise self-play and prevent strategic cycling. Shaped rewards for the first ~20M
  steps, then win/loss only. Networks grew 8 → 16 → 24 blocks with the previous model as teacher. Game phase was an input
  feature; 180° rotation at test time; handcrafted rules at test time for action ordering and conflict resolution. They
  "abandoned their initial rules-based agent after the RL approach outperformed it within the first month". Compute: a
  personal dual-GPU PC, training "overnight most nights". Weaknesses: strategic cycling in pure self-play, poor play when
  far behind, limited robustness to diverse opponents. — [IsaiahPressman/Kaggle_Lux_AI_2021](https://github.com/IsaiahPressman/Kaggle_Lux_AI_2021);
  [Kaggle writeup title](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/lux-ai-2021/writeups/toad-brigade-toad-brigade-s-approach-deep-reinforc)
- Toad Brigade was the "1st team" and their battle records were the training data for imitation learners. "Unit-level neural
  networks work quite well for imitation learning since they imitate coordinated policy from Toad Brigade." —
  [Epicato/lux-AI](https://github.com/Epicato/lux-AI) (snippet). An aggregator claims the top two S1 solutions were "Team
  All You Need and Team Tobright"; this conflicts with the primary sources and looks unreliable. —
  [toolify.ai](https://www.toolify.ai/ai-news/lux-ai-season-1-grand-finale-unveiling-the-top-solutions-and-season-2-preview-2386553)
- S1 imitation-learning results: a U-Net imitation team ("No face") scored 1436.3, rank 93/1178 —
  [benmerrilll/Lux_AI_Kaggle](https://github.com/benmerrilll/Lux_AI_Kaggle) (snippet). shmyak-ai's imitation agents (match
  records via the Kaggle API) plus RL self-play agents placed 19th/1178 — [shmyak-ai/lux-ai](https://github.com/shmyak-ai/lux-ai).
  A HandyRL-extended off-policy RL agent (V-trace/UPGO, 500k episodes, single GPU) beat an imitation baseline (~1350) 75%
  after 800 epochs, but the author confirmed convergence only "1 or half day" before the deadline —
  [Fkaneko/kaggle_lux_ai](https://github.com/Fkaneko/kaggle_lux_ai).
- S2 1st place (ry_andy): 1st of 646 teams; the GitHub repo has code only. The writeup is on Kaggle and not readable
  here. — [ryandy/Lux-S2-public](https://github.com/ryandy/Lux-S2-public); [Kaggle writeup](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/lux-ai-season-2/writeups/ry-andy-1st-place-solution)
- S2 2nd place "Yet-another-logic-bot": a stateful rule-based bot in TypeScript. Each robot gets a "role" (e.g. mining along
  a chain), limited internal forward projection, and most development was done with self-play testing. S2 4th place (FLG):
  "Deep Reinforcement Learning with a Focus on Performance". — [Kaggle S2 discussion](https://www.kaggle.com/c/lux-ai-season-2/discussion/405476) (snippet);
  [FLG 4th place writeup](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/lux-ai-season-2/writeups/flg-flg-s-approach-deep-reinforcement-learning-wit) (title only)
- S3: 1st place Flat Neurons: IMPALA, ~200M parameters, 3–4 days on 8xH100, with ConvLSTM + Transformer layers. 2nd place
  Frog Parade: PPO, two 300M-parameter models, 8 days on RTX3090 + RTX2070Super, environment rewritten in Rust for speed.
  "The strongest solutions employed reinforcement learning and imitation learning"; the best rule-based entry finished
  20th. The 1st place "tried various auxiliary rewards but ultimately adopted only match outcomes". Self-play against a mix
  of current and past models, some with Prioritized Fictitious Self-Play. The summary's author (bronze) switched to a rule
  bot one week before the deadline after RL failed to converge; regrets: Stable-Baselines3, dropping parallelism, over-
  engineered rewards. — [Zenn, kurupical Lux S3 RL summary](https://zenn.dev/kurupical/articles/61dbeedf89a29d)
- Inconsistency in that summary: it gives ~10M training steps for 1st/2nd, but elsewhere says top teams collected
  "200–300M training steps". The units may differ (learner updates vs env frames); unresolved. — [same](https://zenn.dev/kurupical/articles/61dbeedf89a29d)
- Frog Parade members also won S1. Another S3 team imitation-learned from Frog Parade and Flat Neurons replays with a
  weighted cross-entropy (class imbalance) and two U-Nets over a reconstructed world model. — search snippets of
  [Kaggle Frog Parade writeup](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/lux-ai-season-3/writeups/frog-parade-frog-parade-s-solution)
  and [andreyd41/lux3-bot](https://deepwiki.com/andreyd41/lux3-bot)
- S3 design: partial observability and hidden, randomised game parameters, played as best-of-5 on the same map to reward
  in-series adaptation. — [NeurIPS competition page](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/competition/84798)

### Inferences
- In Lux, ML overtook rules when (a) the team could simulate millions of games cheaply (fast env, often rewritten), (b) the
  reward was the true win/loss after an early shaping phase, and (c) self-play used past-model pools or teacher-KL to stop
  cycling. S2 shows rules still win when the game rewards precise long-horizon planning and ML teams lack that throughput.
- Imitating the top team's replays reliably gets to the upper-middle leaderboard (S1: ranks ~19 to ~93 of 1178) but not
  first. This fits Viper's experience: IL gave the big jump, and further IL variants plateaued.
- Toad Brigade's "KL to a frozen teacher" is directly relevant to Viper's PPO failures. Their fine-tuning was anchored to
  the previous best network, as in Viper's KL 0.2–1.0 runs, but at far greater scale (tens of millions of steps).

### Gaps
- S2 1st place method (ry_andy) not confirmed from the primary writeup (Kaggle JS page). From memory of community
  discussion it was rule-based, but this is **unverified**.
- Exact S1 imitation-learning top rank not found (claims of a "9th place hybrid" appeared only in a search summary without a
  source).

## Q4. Halite I–IV (Two Sigma) and Kore 2022

### Takeaway
Halite winners were rule-based or heuristic-optimisation bots. Halite III's winner (teccles) attributes success mainly to
**replay analysis and systematic local testing against previous versions**, with no ML. The Halite IV winner (Tom Van de
Wiele) kept both rule-based and deep-learning folders, and his Kaggle discussion is titled around the rule-based strategy.
Kore 2022 top writeups found were rule-based; one team published an autoregressive imitation-learning approach.

### Cited Findings
- Halite III winner teccles: "Rule-based with algorithmic optimization"; no ML. Dijkstra-based square scoring (travel +
  mining + return time), ownership-based collision risk, dropoff planning, different spawn logic for 2p/4p. "Possibly the key
  skill in this game was analysing replays"; tracked statistics across game types, tested locally against previous
  versions, and rewrote Python → Java to make local games faster. — [teccles-halite/halite3-bot](https://github.com/teccles-halite/halite3-bot)
- Halite IV (Kaggle 2020) 1st place: repo "Kaggle Halite RL challenge – Provisional first place solution" with separate
  rule-based and deep-learning agent folders; the README points to the Kaggle forum "for a discussion of the rule based
  strategy". — [ttvand/Halite](https://github.com/ttvand/Halite); [Kaggle discussion 183543](https://www.kaggle.com/c/halite/discussion/183543)
- Halite IV 4th place was also published (0Zeta). — [0Zeta/HaliteIV-Bot](https://github.com/0Zeta/HaliteIV-Bot) (snippet; approach not read)
- Participation scale: Halite I ~1,500 players; Halite II ~6,000; Halite III 4,000+. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halite_AI_Programming_Competition)
- Kore 2022: 4th place "rule based" with "major strategic changes and code re-writes"; an autoregressive-imitation
  approach was published by another team. — [qihuazhong/kore-2022](https://github.com/qihuazhong/kore-2022) (snippet);
  [khanhvu207/kore2022](https://github.com/khanhvu207/kore2022) (snippet); 1st place thread exists at
  [Kaggle 340035](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/kore-2022/discussion/340035) (not readable)

### Inferences
- In resource-management games with long horizons and exact arithmetic (Halite, Kore, Lux S2), well-engineered rules plus
  good analysis tooling still won as of 2020–2023. ML won when throughput was huge (Lux S1/S3, Hungry Geese, GRF).

### Gaps
- Halite I (erdman) and Halite II winners' approaches not verified this session.
- Halite IV: whether the final winning submission used any DL component is not stated in what could be read.
- Kore 2022 1st place approach not retrieved.

## Q5. MIT Battlecode top-team postmortems: what separated top teams

### Takeaway
Across postmortems, the consistent differentiators were: **heavy replay analysis of top teams and fast adoption of their
strategies ("copy, a lot")**, one clear game philosophy, testing each change individually against old versions on many maps
and both sides, targeted test maps for specific features, and robust reusable primitives (pathing, comms). Clever
algorithms and bytecode optimisation mattered less than expected. The 2025 runner-up says he did almost no bytecode
optimisation.

### Cited Findings
- confused (Michael Hahn, Battlecode 2025, 1st seed, final runner-up, solo, joined after Sprint 1): "extensive replay
  analysis, borrowing strategies that looked good from other teams, and a focus on aligning all development with a
  well-defined core game philosophy"; "I didn't really do any bytecode optimization". Reused pathfinding from the 2024 top
  team (unrolled Bellman-Ford + bug nav) but warns not to copy blindly. Copying Gone Whalin's "tower flickering" gave "a
  70%+ win rate against my submission for qualifiers". Scrims vs other finalists were "consistently close, barely
  maintaining a 50% win rate". The final was lost on one map type (large, corner ruins) his exploration handled badly. — [Battlecode 2025 postmortem, confused](https://battlecode.org/assets/files/postmortem-2025-confused.pdf)
- XSquare (Ivan Geffner, multiple-time finalist) "Guide to Battlecode", Dos and Don'ts: "Copy, a lot. All top teams in
  Battlecode copy from one another"; "Test against old versions… on the largest possible map set… test on both sides";
  "Don't add too many upgrades at once"; design **feature-specific maps** (small open maps for micro, mazes for pathing)
  because a partial change barely moves the overall win rate; "Don't be hasty to discard upgrades: if the winrate is close
  to 50% it might be just bad luck… check for bugs"; "The simple the better… if some global functionality can be
  approximated by independent behavior from each robot, this is usually the way to go"; don't bytecode-optimise early;
  ideal team is one coder plus people watching replays and testing; "Don't overfit for current maps". — [XSquare, A Guide to Battlecode](https://battlecode.org/assets/files/battlecode-guide-xsquare.pdf)
- XSquare 2019 postmortem: the meta cycled (Rush > greedier/cheesier eco > greedy/cheesy > eco > rush). A spec change (castle
  buffs) took their eco bot "from F-tier to S-tier in 1 day"; easy strategies were quickly copied by many teams; "experience
  has told me that consistency is really valuable". — [Battlecode 2019 postmortem, XSquare](https://battlecode.org/assets/files/postmortem-2019-oak.pdf)
- Battlecode 2021: babyducks won; their "slanderer turtle" was replicated by many teams and became the meta by the end of
  week 2. — [Battlecode past years](https://battlecode.org/past.html) (snippet); [Stone Tao BC21 postmortem](https://blog.stoneztao.com/posts/bc21/) (snippet; full page reset the connection)
- Other postmortems available but not read this session: [2025 The Kragle](https://battlecode.org/assets/files/postmortem-2025-the-kragle.pdf),
  [2025 om nom](https://battlecode.org/assets/files/postmortem-2025-om-nom.pdf), [2025 SPAARK](https://battlecode.org/assets/files/postmortem-2025-spaark.pdf),
  [2023 Don't @ Me](https://battlecode.org/assets/files/postmortem-2023-dont-at-me.pdf), [2021 wololo](https://battlecode.org/assets/files/postmortem-2021-wololo.pdf).

### Inferences
- For Viper, the Battlecode lessons point to: (1) targeted test maps per feature, e.g. maze maps for trapped-drone
  openings and endgame maps vs a single giant, instead of general 40-game sandboxes where ±8% noise hides small changes;
  (2) systematic copying of specific behaviours from top teams' replays (S.2's sprint discipline, top teams' 2+2 bud at round
  4, queen consolidation) as rule changes, alongside the network; (3) checking both sides/seat orders.
- The "barely 50% vs other finalists" figure shows that at the top, head-to-head margins are thin. Ladder A/B noise for
  Viper is expected, not a sign that changes don't matter.

### Gaps
- Postmortems by "Producing Perfection", "Chop Suey" and "teh devs" were not located; babyducks' own postmortem not read.

## Q6. Google Research Football (Kaggle 2020) and microRTS

### Takeaway
In GRF, the 1st place (WeKick) combined RL with imitation (GAIL) and AlphaStar-style league training. The 3rd place
(Raw Beast) did **behaviour cloning from leaderboard submissions, then IMPALA RL** with half the games vs self and half vs a
pool of ~10 diverse opponents. A pure-PPO self-play team needed 370 hours and 450k episodes to reach 6th.

### Cited Findings
- WeKick (1st): "imitation learning, the multi-head value trick, and distributed league training"; the opponent pool was
  initialised with RL and GAIL strategies; PPO with LSTM (256 hidden, 32 steps). — [Springer MIR empirical study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11633-023-1426-8) (snippet);
  [garnote.com summary](https://garnote.com/archives/2374)
- Raw Beast (3rd): behaviour cloning from leaderboard submissions + IMPALA; "Half of the experience… comes from self-play"
  and half from "a big (about 10) pool of diverse opponent strategies"; x-axis symmetry augmentation; critic LR 2x actor
  LR; transfer across ~20 submission variants. — [garnote.com summary](https://garnote.com/archives/2374)
- liveinparis (6th): pure self-play PPO with action masking; "370 hours" on 30 CPU cores + 1 GPU; "450,000 episodes";
  opponent pool ~380 models, 50% from the latest 10. — [garnote.com summary](https://garnote.com/archives/2374)
- HandyRL (DeNA) placed 5th in GRF. — [DeNA/HandyRL](https://github.com/DeNA/HandyRL)
- TiKick (post-competition) built a full-game multi-agent football AI from single-agent demonstrations (WeKick replays),
  i.e. imitation from the winner's replays followed by further training. — [TiKick arXiv 2110.04507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.04507)

### Inferences
- GRF repeats the Hungry Geese and Lux pattern: **BC from the top leaderboard, then RL against a diverse opponent pool**,
  with the BC policy as the starting point and some anchor to it. Pure self-play gets there only with very large compute.

### Gaps
- SaltyFish (2nd) details not retrieved. microRTS competition results not researched (time budget).

## Q7. Cross-cutting: when ML beats heuristic bots, and how teams used top-player replays

### Takeaway
ML beat rules where (1) simulation was cheap enough for 10^6–10^7+ self-play games, (2) the team started early (Toad
Brigade dropped rules within a month; HandyRL led from the monthly prizes), and (3) search was added at play time for
tactics. Top-player replays were used everywhere as a **starting policy (BC)** or an **opponent/meta snapshot**, then
improved with RL or search. Pure imitation of the leaders plateaued below the leaders. Pure self-play without anchors
cycled (Toad Brigade) or hit local optima (Hungry Geese 21st). Offline evaluation noise was the universal bottleneck.

### Cited Findings
- RL overtook the team's own rules within the first month (Lux S1). — [IsaiahPressman/Kaggle_Lux_AI_2021](https://github.com/IsaiahPressman/Kaggle_Lux_AI_2021)
- Rule-based entries topped out around 20th in Lux S3; RL/IL took the top. — [Zenn kurupical](https://zenn.dev/kurupical/articles/61dbeedf89a29d)
- Rules won Lux S2 2nd place (and reportedly 1st), Halite III, and Kore 4th. — [Kaggle S2 discussion](https://www.kaggle.com/c/lux-ai-season-2/discussion/405476) (snippet); [teccles](https://github.com/teccles-halite/halite3-bot); [qihuazhong/kore-2022](https://github.com/qihuazhong/kore-2022) (snippet)
- Replay-based IL used as stage 1 (Hungry Geese 3rd and 11th; GRF 3rd; Lux S1 and S3 IL teams). — sources above
- Opponent diversity was stressed repeatedly: HandyRL's final phase vs past models and aggressive bots; Raw Beast 50%
  pool; liveinparis 380-model pool; S3 PFSP. — sources above
- Evaluation: Hungry Geese 3rd place used 224 CPUs just for evaluation; the 21st-place author called offline evaluation
  "nearly impossible with limited resources". — [Speaker Deck](https://speakerdeck.com/hoxomaxwell/kaggle-hungry-geese); [Zenn](https://zenn.dev/ktechb/articles/e2394bc27358c4)

### Inferences (for Viper, Battlecode 2026)
- Viper's hybrid (C++ safety rules + BC prior) matches the "BC then something" first stage every successful ML team used.
  The missing second stage in the winners was **search** (Hungry Geese 1st/3rd/11th) or **very large RL with teacher-KL and
  opponent pools** (Lux S1/S3, GRF). Given the team's compute (a laptop plus free Kaggle/Colab), network-guided local
  search (decoupled UCT or paranoid depth-2/3 over nearby dragon heads, network log-probs as priors or rollout policy) is
  the most transferable idea.
- The Hungry Geese 3rd-place "expert iteration" (imitate episodes generated by the **search-augmented** agent, mixed with
  current top-meta episodes) is a principled fix for Viper's failed self-imitation. There, the network learned to replace
  C++ safety logic. Imitating the search/safety-augmented bot's chosen moves, labelled as such, would avoid this.
- Battlecode-specific evidence says the biggest remaining gains usually come from copying specific top-team behaviours
  after replay analysis (e.g. early swarm density, queen consolidation), each tested alone on targeted maps.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies how often a hybrid "rules + small BC prior" (Viper's exact design) won a competition. The closest
  are Toad Brigade's test-time rules on an RL net and Hungry Geese's NN + MCTS ensembles.
