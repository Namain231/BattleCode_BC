# Spend the spare CPU on search, not self-imitation

The literature and the competition record support one claim, and the team's own results already point to it. A behaviour-cloned prior is a strong first stage, and the Y.7.12-nn.1 jump (24/40 vs 6/40 on the ladder) is the typical size of that first-stage gain. In comparable games, the second stage that won was either **search guided by the prior** (Hungry Geese 1st, 3rd and 11th, and every strong Battlesnake) or **RL at millions of games, anchored to a teacher and trained against opponent pools** (Lux S1 and S3, Google Research Football). In each case the network learned from something stronger than itself. The bot's rule `score + 2·log p` is, mathematically, greedy piKL with λ = 2 in heuristic-score units. That makes three levers the cheapest untested ones: the weight's ratio to the score's scale, a weight that changes with the state, and a 1–2-ply lookahead over the top 2–3 candidate moves, which fits in the ~40M instructions of safe CPU headroom. The in-flight experiment retrains the network on the full bot's own winning moves. Expert-iteration and distillation theory predict it sits close to a fixed point: where the heuristic scores are flat, the label is the network's own argmax, and where they are not, the heuristic gets counted twice at play time. Its one real upside is coverage of unseen `maps_gen` layouts. The binding constraint, though, is measurement. **Forty games carry a standard error of 7.9 points, so a "noise-level" result was never a measurement, and a 55%-over-40-games gate passes about one neutral change in three.** At ~480 local games an hour, properly powered paired SPRTs are affordable. The plan below ranks the next steps and gives each a pre-registered progress rule and turn-back rule.

## The bot's move rule is already greedy piKL at λ = 2

AlphaZero's PUCT and Meta's piKL both describe what "heuristic + w·log prior" does, and they describe it exactly. Grill et al. prove that the AlphaZero selection rule approximately solves `argmax_y qᵀy − λ_N·KL(π_prior, y)`. The prior's weight λ_N decays roughly as 1/√N as visits accumulate, so the prior dominates when there is little evidence and fades when search has evaluated the moves ([Grill et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12509)). piKL makes the same point for greedy choice. **`argmax_a Q(a) + λ·log τ(a)` is the mode of the KL-regularised optimum `π ∝ τ·exp(Q/λ)`**, where τ is an imitation-learned "anchor" ([Jacob et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07544)). Viper's move choice is therefore piKL, with the linear heuristic score as Q, the CNN as the anchor, λ = 2 and zero search depth.

This framing yields four concrete consequences.

**Only the ratio of w to the score's spread matters.** Meta found that "the scale of λ is related to the scale of the payoffs in the game" ([Jacob et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07544)). Gumbel MuZero normalises Q to [0,1] before mixing it with logits ([mctx](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mctx)). If Viper's score spread among safe moves grows late in the game (pearl values, queen feeding), the network's effective influence shrinks without anyone changing w.

**Temperature and w are the same knob for greedy selection.** Rescaling the network's softmax only changes w/T. So calibration, and the offline ensembles and TTA that the team already found worth ≤ +1.4%, cannot matter unless w becomes state-dependent or log p is floored.

**PUCT's own logic says the weight should shrink where the heuristic has strong evidence** (a large score gap, a clear tactical danger) and grow where the heuristic is flat or blind (ties, open areas, unseen enemies).

**The optimal regularisation depends on the opponent population.** In no-press Diplomacy, λ = 1e-1 roughly kept the imitation model's accuracy and scored **1.4× the anchor**. Mild λ = 1e-3 was best in a mixed 8-agent population (SoS 32.9%), and "performance drops as we continue to increase λ past 1e-3" ([Jacob et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07544)). w = 2 has never been swept. The single measured effect of the network, 6/40 → 24/40, says the response curve around it is steep.

The same papers show what adding depth is worth. An imitation-only chess model (Maia1900) plus MCTS with 50 playouts beats the raw model **72–94%** depending on c_puct, and the weakest prior (smallest c_puct) plays strongest ([Jacob et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07544)). In Hex, network-guided MCTS beat plain MCTS 97% despite a 2× slowdown, while doubling plain MCTS iterations gained only 56% ([Anthony et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)).

The usual objection is that the budget allows very few simulations. Gumbel AlphaZero answers it: it guarantees policy improvement with as few as 2 simulations. It samples root actions without replacement and picks `argmax g + logit + σ(q̂)` ([Danihelka et al. 2022](https://mlanthology.org/iclr/2022/danihelka2022iclr-policy/); [ICLR slides](https://iclr.cc/media/iclr-2022/Slides/6418.pdf)). A dragon has at most three non-reversing moves, so "evaluate every root action" is trivially affordable, and the failure mode Gumbel fixes (unvisited actions) never arises. The open question is different and has not been answered in public. No published result covers shallow search with a BC prior under heavy partial observability and per-unit control, and enemy heads outside the 7×7 view are unknown. Diplomacy is the closest analogue, and there the imitation policy doubled as an **opponent model**. Viper could do the same: run the CNN from a visible enemy head's point of view to replace worst-case head-on assumptions. At ~18M instructions per forward pass, the sandbox's 65M ceiling allows this for only one or two visible enemies.

If RL is ever revisited, residual policy learning names the right parameterisation. Learn a zero-initialised correction on top of a fixed controller, `π = π_0 + f_θ`, and train the critic alone first. RPL "should never make a good initial policy worse", yet it still "drops early in training before quickly recovering" even with critic burn-in. It gave nearly 10× data efficiency over learning from scratch ([Silver et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06298)). For Viper, that means residual logits added to `S/λ + log p`, masked to the safety class and KL-anchored to the champion rather than to the raw network.

## Winning hybrids paired the prior with search or with million-game RL

The competitions closest to this game all follow the same pattern. Imitation of the leaders gets a team to the upper-middle of the table. What separates winners is either search at play time or RL at a scale this team does not have.

The compute gap settles part of the question. The team's six PPO runs were orders of magnitude below HandyRL's 19M games or the GRF sixth place's 450k episodes. Their failure is consistent with scale, not proof that RL cannot work here, and it is also a reason not to expect RL to become affordable. Search needs no training throughput. It spends the ~75M instructions per dragon-turn the bot leaves unused (~40M within the sandbox's 65M safety cap), and it is the one ingredient every top Hungry Geese entry and every strong Battlesnake shares. Both the Hungry Geese 21st-place and 3rd-place writeups also identify offline evaluation as the universal bottleneck: "high variance in win counts across trials and complex matchup dynamics made offline evaluation nearly impossible with limited resources", which led to "ineffective micro-tweaks" ([Zenn](https://zenn.dev/ktechb/articles/e2394bc27358c4)). The team's record of noise-level tweaks is the same symptom.

## Retraining on the bot's own wins mostly teaches the network to echo itself

The failed network-only self-imitation (16/42 in the bot, even when network played network) is textbook distribution shift combined with causal confusion. Behaviour cloning compounds errors quadratically in the horizon when the learner's states differ from the expert's ([Ross et al. 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686)). Discriminative imitators latch onto whatever best predicts the target action, so "access to more information can give worse performance" ([de Haan et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11979)). In network-only play, avoiding death was visible to the network only through its own move targets, so it spent capacity learning a stand-in for the C++ safety filter. Inside the bot, that filter already exists, and the network's real job, ranking safe moves, got worse. The same body of work predicted the team's flat results with the memory plane and previous-turn input. When actions are temporally correlated, history-conditioned imitators "learn to cheat by predicting the expert's previous action" ([Wen et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14876)).

The in-flight experiment fixes the state distribution, because states now come from the deployed bot as DAgger prescribes. The labels, however, are the bot's own choices, `argmax S + 2·log p`, and that creates two separate problems. Where S is flat among safe moves, the label is the network's own argmax, so training sharpens the prior toward its current mode with the same errors. Where S decides the move, the network learns to reproduce S, and at play time S is added again, so the heuristic is counted twice. Expert Iteration improves only when the expert is strictly stronger than the apprentice through information the apprentice lacks. Even then, the target matters. **Distilling the search distribution rather than the chosen action made the network 50 ± 13 Elo stronger at equal top-1 accuracy** ([Anthony et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)). Grill et al. warn that AlphaZero "entangles search and learning, which is not desirable", and recommend training on π̄, the improved distribution computed from Q ([Grill et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12509)). No published study covers exactly Viper's additive heuristic-plus-network composite, so the double-counting argument is an inference, though a direct one.

The winners-only filter adds further bias. Viper's own win predictor calls the winner 72% of the time at round 50, and the mirror policy plays both sides. Once the opening decides a game, winners' later moves are mostly "moves typical of being ahead", and in stochastic settings "trajectories that result in a return may have only achieved that return due to luck" ([Paster et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15967)). The principled alternatives weight each move by an advantage rather than filtering whole games. Self-Imitation Learning uses `max(R − V(s), 0)` ([Oh et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.05635)), and AWR uses `exp(A/β)` ([Peng et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00177)). Both keep losers' good moves and discount winners' moves in positions that were already won.

The 50/50 mix is the part of the design that is on safe ground. Training on a model's own outputs *in place of* real data makes "tails of the original content distribution disappear" ([Shumailov et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17493)). *Accumulating* synthetic data alongside the original data keeps error bounded however many iterations run ([Gerstgrasser et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01413)). For Viper, the tails are the situations only top opponents create: single-giant endgames, sonar tricks, portal play. Replay data must stay at 50% or more in every retrain and must never be dropped.

The steelman for the in-flight run is real. Half the self-play games are on `maps_gen`, and the next phase uses new maps, so coverage of unseen layouts could help even if the double-counting makes the run neutral on known maps. The evaluation must therefore report live maze maps and generated maps separately.

The same literature explains the PPO "dip first" symptom. PIRLNav found that "naively finetuning the actor with a randomly-initialized critic leads to a rapid drop in performance". Its fix combined critic-only training, a decaying critic learning rate and an actor learning rate warmed up from 0, and it lifted success from 52.0% to 61.9%, against +1.6% for naive fine-tuning. It also found that gains shrink as behaviour-cloning accuracy rises ([PIRLNav](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07302)). Wolczyk et al. trace fine-tuning failures to forgetting of states the new policy stops visiting, and find a BC loss on old data the strongest remedy ([Wolczyk et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02868)). The team's `--critic-warmup` flag covers only one of these fixes.

Offline metrics will not settle the in-flight test. Offline accuracy and network-vs-network results (29/56) both failed to predict the in-bot result (16/42). Four diagnostics track the failure modes above, and none of them is a verdict:

- accuracy on held-out strong-team replays, restricted to safe moves (a collapse detector);
- agreement with the heuristic's argmax (rising agreement warns of double counting);
- entropy of p over the safe set (falling entropy warns of mode-sharpening);
- replay-validation loss.

## Forty games cannot see anything smaller than a 70% win rate

With no draws, the per-game standard deviation is 0.5, and the resolution follows directly from the normalised-Elo framework ([Van den Bergh](https://cantate.be/Fishtest/normalized_elo_practical.pdf)). The figures below are derived from it.

| Games | 1 SE | 95% half-width | Smallest true win rate detectable (80% power, two-sided α = 0.05) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | 7.9% | 15.5% | ≈ 72% |
| 100 | 5.0% | 9.8% | ≈ 64% |
| 400 | 2.5% | 4.9% | ≈ 57% |

The team's "±8% at 40 games" is one standard error, not a confidence interval. The results 20/40, 17/40 and 21/40 therefore cannot rule out true effects of ±10–15 points: they are *unmeasured*, not *neutral*. The same arithmetic cuts the other way. Viper's 16/42 for network-only self-imitation is 1.5 SE below the 21/42 control (one-sided p ≈ 0.06). That is suggestive of harm but not proof. Two process problems follow from these numbers. First, **the gate "≥ 55% over 40 games" passes a truly neutral change with probability ≈ 32%**. Second, testing k neutral tweaks at α = 0.05 yields at least one false pass 40% of the time at k = 10, and the best of ten neutral 40-game tests shows about 62%. Fishtest reinforces the point: accepting H0 "only means the SPRT failed to demonstrate the expected gain" ([coeusyk](https://github.com/coeusyk/chess-engine/issues/231)).

Fishtest's expected SPRT length is about 1,000,000/(width in normalised Elo)² games in the worst case at α = β = 0.05 ([Van den Bergh](https://cantate.be/Fishtest/normalized_elo_practical.pdf)). Fishtest itself runs a cheap short-time-control screen before an expensive long-time-control confirmation ([Fishtest wiki](https://github.com/official-stockfish/fishtest/wiki/Creating-my-first-test)). Translated to this game (derived; α = β = 0.1 cuts each figure to ~56%):

| SPRT bounds | Win-rate question | Worst-case games | Games if truth = H0 or H1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| [0, 100] Elo | 50% vs 64% | ≈ 105 | ≈ 65 |
| [0, 70] Elo | 50% vs 60% | ≈ 215 | ≈ 130 |
| [0, 35] Elo | 50% vs 55% | ≈ 850 | ≈ 520 |

At the documented local throughput of 40 games in about five minutes, even the [0, 35] test is roughly a two-hour job. The team has been under-powering tests by habit, not by necessity.

Pairing is the cheapest variance reduction. Fishtest's pentanomial model scores colour-reversed pairs as a unit, and in chess the within-pair correlation of about −0.15 saves ~15% of games ([Fishtest issue #348](https://github.com/official-stockfish/fishtest/issues/348); [Fishtest maths](https://official-stockfish.github.io/docs/fishtest-wiki/Fishtest-Mathematics.html)). In this game, strong map and side effects could make the correlation more negative and the saving larger. A candidate that wins or loses whole maps from both sides makes it positive, and then a naive Bernoulli SPRT *over*-accepts. The correlation should be measured from existing tourney logs, and the SPRT run on pair scores. Because the next phase uses new maps, the effective sample size for generalisation is closer to the number of distinct maps than to the number of games. Many `maps_gen` maps played once per side beat many seeds on ten maps, and repeating a deterministic seed adds nothing.

Two further effects explain why sandbox gains often fail to transfer. Real games have a large cyclic, rock-paper-scissors component, largest at medium skill ([Czarnecki et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.09468)), and a self-play agent "may forget how to win against a previous version of itself" ([DeepMind AlphaStar](https://deepmind.google/blog/article/AlphaStar-Grandmaster-level-in-StarCraft-II-using-multi-agent-reinforcement-learning)). The remedy is a frozen gauntlet: older V7.x builds, teammates' S.2 and vB styles, a swarm bot and a single-giant bot. Results should be reported per opponent and weighted by the ladder's opponent mix, or by Nash averaging, which "automatically adapts to redundancies" ([Balduzzi et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02643)). The ladder is the noisiest instrument of all (figures derived):

- A 30-vs-30 ladder A/B has a standard error of ~12.9 points on the difference.
- Detecting 10 points of difference needs ~390 battles per arm.
- For single-game battles at K = 24, the stationary rating noise is ~46 Elo, so 1664 → 1711 is not by itself evidence. The per-build win rate is the better metric.
- The ladder should serve as a *paired non-regression check*, analysed on discordant pairs (McNemar), never as the place to discover 5-point edges.

The UNSW ladder's games-per-battle format is undocumented, and the rating-noise figure depends on it.

For stopping research directions, the evidence is frameworks, not measured studies. Hamming's test is whether "you have a reasonable attack" ([Hamming](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html)). Kaggle grandmasters say "the biggest lever in any competition is the number of high-quality experiments you can run" ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/kaggle-grandmasters-unveil-winning-strategies-for-data-science-superpowers/)). Schulman warns against spending "months on a 10% tweak with no larger target of value" ([summary](https://braindump.jethro.dev/posts/an_opinionated_guide_to_ml_research/)). Operationally, each direction gets written down in advance:

- the hypothesis;
- the metric (a stage-2 SPRT result);
- a budget (games, cloud sessions, calendar days);
- a kill rule, typically two full-power H0 acceptances for the best variant, or the time box expiring.

Underpowered 40-game tests count as zero pulls of a direction, not as failures. Near the deadline α should fall, because a false promotion cannot be undone, while β can rise. Re-activating 9285 is always a valid fallback.

## Conclusion

Viper's plateau looks like a missing-teacher problem, not a data or network-size problem. The imitation teams in Lux S1 plateaued below the team they copied, and ExIt improves only when the apprentice learns from something stronger than itself. Every self-imitation variant the team has tried used a teacher no stronger than the student. The only affordable stronger teacher is search, and it happens to use a resource the bot leaves idle: roughly three-quarters of the per-turn instruction budget. That reorders the agenda. Build search first as a play-time improvement, and only then use it as the labeller that makes further network training worthwhile.

The record is also less settled than the "what has been tried" table implies. The network-variant family has a coherent explanation for its failures and an offline ceiling, so parking it is justified. Most heuristic tweaks, however, were tested at a resolution that could not detect anything below ~70%, so they are unmeasured rather than refuted. A positive control and pair-scored SPRTs at a few hundred games cost an afternoon. They would turn the next month of experiments from noise into evidence, and XSquare's warning against hastily discarding upgrades near 50% applies with full force.
